Graduates drive the 2026 work permit rebound, but B.C.’s share shrinks

Mohsen Fazeli · October 19, 2026

Summary

This note uses Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) work permit data [1, 2], analysed in the accompanying dashboard [13], to inform B.C. labour market and skills planning, including the B.C. Labour Market Outlook [3].

- Permits are rebounding, but the temporary population is not. Permits rose 46% in January–July 2026 from a year earlier and, over 12 months, are back to 95% of their peak, yet Statistics Canada estimates temporary residents fell [12].
- Graduates drive the rebound. Post-graduation work permits (PGWPs) supplied 85% of the increase.
- Employer-sponsored permits are falling faster in B.C. Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) permits fell 15% in B.C. over the same months, against 5% nationally.
- B.C.’s share of the rebound is small. It supplied 12% of the increase, though it held 18% of permits a year earlier.

For B.C.: growth now rests on graduates, whose routes to permanent residence have narrowed [10]. How many stay is the key planning question.

Background

The TFWP lets employers hire foreign workers, usually after a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) shows no Canadian is available; this note calls these permits employer-sponsored. The International Mobility Program (IMP) needs no LMIA; it covers PGWPs for graduates of Canadian institutions [11], spouses and youth exchanges.

The data, from IRCC’s open data release of September 15, 2026, count permits that took effect each month, by intended province, from January 2015 to July 2026 [1, 2]. January–July 2026 is compared with the same months of 2025. Figures are rounded; exact values are in Appendix D.

Findings

Permits more than tripled, fell, and are climbing again

Permits rose from 265,000 in 2015 to 970,000 in 2023, dipping only in 2020, when COVID-19 travel restrictions took effect [4]. Over 12-month periods, they peaked at 1.02 million in the year to March 2024, then fell to 755,000 in 2025. The decline followed a federal target to lower temporary residents’ share of the population [5] and tighter study permit, PGWP and TFWP rules [6–8], although the data show timing, not cause.

In January–July 2026, 673,000 permits took effect, 46% more than a year earlier and above the 2024 high for these months (Figure 1). Yet permits are not people: Statistics Canada estimates that non-permanent residents fell by 155,000 in the year to July 1, 2026, led by study permit holders [12].

Graduates lift permits; employer-sponsored permits fall

In January–July, PGWPs rose from 86,000 to 266,000, supplying 180,000 of the 212,000 additional permits (Figure C3). The cause is untested; larger student cohorts admitted before the 2024 study permit cap [6] may now be graduating. The rise is real, not a recoding: permits with no identified program (“Unknown IMP”) also rose, by 26,000.

Employer-sponsored permits moved the other way. Non-agricultural LMIA permits more than tripled from 2021 to 2024, after low-wage caps were raised in 2022 [9]. They then fell 21% in 2025 and another 15% in January–July 2026, after the 2024 low-wage limits [7].

B.C.’s share of the rebound shrinks

B.C. supplied 12% of the national increase, below its 18% share of permits a year earlier (Figure C8); Ontario supplied 53% (Figure 2). Two things explain the gap. B.C.’s PGWPs grew more slowly than Canada’s (176% against 208%), and its other permits fell by 1,500 while Canada’s rose by 32,000. All of B.C.’s growth therefore came from graduates.

Employer-sponsored permits are also falling faster in B.C. In January–July, TFWP permits fell 15% in B.C. and 21% in Alberta, but were almost unchanged in Ontario (Figure C5). B.C.’s decline is in non-agricultural LMIA permits (down 32%); agricultural permits held steady (Figure C6). The TFWP is 27% of B.C.’s permits against 22% nationally (2025), so this decline weighs more on B.C.

Implications and next steps for B.C.

- Track temporary labour supply monthly. The Outlook expects temporary residents to flow out of B.C. over 2025–2035 [3]; the falling temporary population fits this, despite rising permits. Reading monthly IRCC permits with Statistics Canada population estimates would show early if supply strays from that path.
- Find out how many graduates stay. The study permit cap may shrink future graduating classes, and routes to permanent residence have narrowed: the 2025 BC Provincial Nominee Program allocation was cut and its international post-graduate stream closed [10]. An IRCC data request for PGWPs by B.C. economic region and institution would show how many graduates stay, and where, helping test whether their skills match high-opportunity occupations.
- Identify sectors losing employer-sponsored workers. IRCC’s TFWP tables by occupation would show which B.C. occupations account for the 32% fall in non-agricultural LMIA permits.

Data notes

A person can hold several permits, and a permit does not show employment. IRCC suppresses values of 0–5 (“--”, counted here as 0) and rounds others to the nearest 5; data are preliminary [1, 2]. “Unknown IMP” made up 20% of permits in 2023, 14% in January–July 2026 and 49% of Alberta’s 2026 increase, so stream comparisons are approximate, especially for Alberta.
