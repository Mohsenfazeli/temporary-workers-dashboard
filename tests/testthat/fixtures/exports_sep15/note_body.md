Data Insight – October 19, 2026

Mohsen Fazeli · October 19, 2026 · IRCC data to July 2026

Summary

Work permits are rebounding, led by graduates, while employer-sponsored permits fall faster in B.C. than nationally. B.C.’s growth now rests on graduates, whose routes to permanent residence have narrowed [10]; how many stay is the key planning question.

- Permits are rebounding, but the temporary population is not. Permits rose 46% in January–July 2026 from the same months of 2025, the highest January–July in the data, yet Statistics Canada estimates temporary residents fell [12].
- Graduates drive the rebound. Post-graduation work permits (PGWPs) supplied 85% of the national increase.
- B.C.’s share of the rebound is small. B.C. held 18% of Canada’s permits a year earlier but supplied 12% of the increase.
- Employer-sponsored permits are falling faster in B.C. Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) permits fell 15% in B.C. over the same months, against 5% nationally.

Background

This note uses Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) work permit data [1, 2], analysed in the accompanying dashboard [13], to inform B.C. labour market and skills planning, including the B.C. Labour Market Outlook [3]. The data, from IRCC’s open data release of September 15, 2026, count permits issued each month, by intended province, from January 2015 to July 2026 [1, 2]. January–July 2026 is compared with the same months of 2025. Policy events are context: the data show timing, not cause. Figures are rounded; exact values are in Appendix D.

The TFWP lets employers hire foreign workers, usually after a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) shows no Canadian is available; this note calls these permits employer-sponsored, split into farm permits (agricultural streams) and non-farm permits (IRCC’s “other workers with an LMIA”). The International Mobility Program (IMP) needs no LMIA; it covers PGWPs for graduates of Canadian institutions [11], spouses and youth exchanges.

Findings

Permits more than tripled, fell, and are climbing again

Permits rose from 265,000 in 2015 to 970,000 in 2023, dipping only in 2020, when COVID-19 travel restrictions took effect [4]. They fell to 755,000 in 2025, after a federal target to lower temporary residents’ share of the population [5] and tighter study permit, PGWP and TFWP rules [6–8].

In January–July 2026, 673,000 permits were issued, 46% more than in the same months of 2025 and the highest January–July in the data (Figure 1). Yet permits are not people: Statistics Canada estimates that non-permanent residents fell by 155,000 in the year to July 1, 2026, led by study permit holders [12]. Permits can rise while people fall, likely because new PGWPs go to graduates of Canadian institutions [11], who were already in Canada as students.

Graduates lift permits; employer-sponsored permits fall

In January–July, PGWPs rose from 86,000 to 266,000. They supplied 180,000 of the 212,000 additional permits. The cause is untested; larger student cohorts admitted before the 2024 study permit cap [6] may now be graduating.

Employer-sponsored permits moved the other way. Nationally, non-farm permits more than tripled from 2021 to 2024, after low-wage caps were raised in 2022 [9]. They then fell 21% from 2024 to 2025 and another 15% in January–July 2026 from a year earlier, after the 2024 low-wage limits [7].

B.C.’s share of the rebound shrinks

B.C. held 18% of Canada’s permits in January–July 2025 but supplied 12% of the increase to 2026. Ontario supplied 53% (Figure 2). Graduates account for more than all of B.C.’s growth; other permits fell (Table 1).

Employer-sponsored permits are also falling faster in B.C. In January–July, TFWP permits fell 15% in B.C., against 5% in Canada (Table 1). They fell 21% in Alberta but were almost unchanged in Ontario. B.C.’s decline is in non-farm permits (down 32%); farm permits held steady. The TFWP was 27% of B.C.’s 2025 permits, against 22% nationally, so this decline weighs more on B.C.

Implications and next steps for B.C.

- Find out how many graduates stay. Graduates account for more than all of B.C.’s growth in 2026 (Table 1). The study permit cap may shrink future graduating classes, and routes to permanent residence have narrowed: the 2025 BC Provincial Nominee Program allocation was cut and its international post-graduate stream closed [10]. An IRCC data request for PGWPs by B.C. economic region and institution would show how many graduates stay, and where, helping test whether their skills match high-opportunity occupations.
- Identify sectors losing employer-sponsored workers. IRCC’s TFWP tables by occupation would show which B.C. occupations account for the 32% fall in non-farm permits.
- Track temporary labour supply monthly. The Outlook expects temporary residents to flow out of B.C. over 2025–2035 [3]; the falling temporary population [12] fits this. Reading monthly IRCC permits with Statistics Canada population estimates would show early if supply strays from that path.

Data notes

A person can hold several permits, and a permit does not show employment. IRCC suppresses values of 0–5 (“--”, counted here as 0) and rounds others to the nearest 5; data are preliminary [1, 2]. Permits with no identified program (“Unknown IMP”) made up 20% of permits in 2023 and 14% in January–July 2026. They supplied 49% of Alberta’s 2026 increase, so stream comparisons are approximate, especially for Alberta. The PGWP rise is not a recoding out of Unknown IMP, which also rose, by 26,000.
