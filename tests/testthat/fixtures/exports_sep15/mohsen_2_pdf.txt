Data Insight – October 19, 2026
Mohsen Fazeli · October 19, 2026

Summary
This note uses Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) work permit data [1, 2], analysed in the
accompanying dashboard [13], to inform B.C. labour market and skills planning, including the B.C. Labour Market
Outlook [3].
  • Permits are rebounding, but the temporary population is not. Permits rose 46% in January–July 2026 from a year
     earlier and, over 12 months, are back to 95% of their peak, yet Statistics Canada estimates temporary residents fell
     [12].
  • Graduates drive the rebound. Post-graduation work permits (PGWPs) supplied 85% of the increase.
  • Employer-sponsored permits are falling faster in B.C. Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP) permits fell
     15% in B.C. over the same months, against 5% nationally.
  • B.C.’s share of the rebound is small. It supplied 12% of the increase, though it held 18% of permits a year earlier.
For B.C.: growth now rests on graduates, whose routes to permanent residence have narrowed [10]. How many stay is
the key planning question.
Background
The TFWP lets employers hire foreign workers, usually after a Labour Market Impact Assessment (LMIA) shows no
Canadian is available; this note calls these permits employer-sponsored. The International Mobility Program (IMP)
needs no LMIA; it covers PGWPs for graduates of Canadian institutions [11], spouses and youth exchanges.
The data, from IRCC’s open data release of September 15, 2026, count permits that took effect each month, by
intended province, from January 2015 to July 2026 [1, 2]. January–July 2026 is compared with the same months of
2025. Figures are rounded; exact values are in Appendix D.
Findings
Permits more than tripled, fell, and are climbing again
Permits rose from 265,000 in 2015 to 970,000 in 2023, dipping only in 2020, when COVID-19 travel restrictions took
effect [4]. Over 12-month periods, they peaked at 1.02 million in the year to March 2024, then fell to 755,000 in 2025.
The decline followed a federal target to lower temporary residents’ share of the population [5] and tighter study
permit, PGWP and TFWP rules [6–8], although the data show timing, not cause.
In January–July 2026, 673,000 permits took effect, 46% more than a year earlier and above the 2024 high for these
months (Figure 1). Yet permits are not people: Statistics Canada estimates that non-permanent residents fell by
155,000 in the year to July 1, 2026, led by study permit holders [12].




Figure 1. Permits that took effect, January–July of each year, Canada. Dashboard Overview tab; filters: Canada, both programs,
Annual view, show only Jan–Jul of each year, Jan 2015 to Jul 2026.
Graduates lift permits; employer-sponsored permits fall
In January–July, PGWPs rose from 86,000 to 266,000, supplying 180,000 of the 212,000 additional permits (Figure C3).
The cause is untested; larger student cohorts admitted before the 2024 study permit cap [6] may now be graduating.
The rise is real, not a recoding: permits with no identified program (“Unknown IMP”) also rose, by 26,000.

Employer-sponsored permits moved the other way. Non-agricultural LMIA permits more than tripled from 2021 to
2024, after low-wage caps were raised in 2022 [9]. They then fell 21% in 2025 and another 15% in January–July 2026,
after the 2024 low-wage limits [7].
B.C.’s share of the rebound shrinks
B.C. supplied 12% of the national increase, below its 18% share of permits a year earlier (Figure C8); Ontario supplied
53% (Figure 2). Two things explain the gap. B.C.’s PGWPs grew more slowly than Canada’s (176% against 208%), and its
other permits fell by 1,500 while Canada’s rose by 32,000. All of B.C.’s growth therefore came from graduates.




Figure 2. Share of Canada’s January–July 2026 increase, by province and territory. Dashboard Geography tab; filters: British
Columbia (highlighted), both programs, period ending July 2026.
Employer-sponsored permits are also falling faster in B.C. In January–July, TFWP permits fell 15% in B.C. and 21% in
Alberta, but were almost unchanged in Ontario (Figure C5). B.C.’s decline is in non-agricultural LMIA permits (down
32%); agricultural permits held steady (Figure C6). The TFWP is 27% of B.C.’s permits against 22% nationally (2025), so
this decline weighs more on B.C.
Implications and next steps for B.C.
    Track temporary labour supply monthly. The Outlook expects temporary residents to flow out of B.C. over 2025–
     2035 [3]; the falling temporary population fits this, despite rising permits. Reading monthly IRCC permits with
     Statistics Canada population estimates would show early if supply strays from that path.
    Find out how many graduates stay. The study permit cap may shrink future graduating classes, and routes to
     permanent residence have narrowed: the 2025 BC Provincial Nominee Program allocation was cut and its
     international post-graduate stream closed [10]. An IRCC data request for PGWPs by B.C. economic region and
     institution would show how many graduates stay, and where, helping test whether their skills match high-
     opportunity occupations.
    Identify sectors losing employer-sponsored workers. IRCC’s TFWP tables by occupation would show which B.C.
     occupations account for the 32% fall in non-agricultural LMIA permits.
Data notes
A person can hold several permits, and a permit does not show employment. IRCC suppresses values of 0–5 (“--”,
counted here as 0) and rounds others to the nearest 5; data are preliminary [1, 2]. “Unknown IMP” made up 20% of
permits in 2023, 14% in January–July 2026 and 49% of Alberta’s 2026 increase, so stream comparisons are
approximate, especially for Alberta.

Appendix A. Method and assumptions
 Data: only the two IRCC CSV files [1, 2], in the IRCC open data release of September 15, 2026 (data to July 2026)
   (files retrieved October 16, 2026), read with the Temporary Workers dashboard code [13], which shows the same
   release, so every IRCC number matches the dashboard. Latest month: July 2026. Background sources are cited for
   context only; the one outside number is Statistics Canada's estimate of the fall in non-permanent residents [12], a
   count of people, not permits.
 Measure: a flow. Each value counts work permits that took effect in a month (IRCC's wording is "became effective":
   the month the permit was signed and given to the person), by intended province or territory. The dashboard calls
   the same counts "work permits issued"; the two terms mean the same thing here. New permits, renewals and
   changes of status all count. Annual figures are sums of months, labelled "permits", not people, and are not the
   number of workers present on a date.
 Part-year: 2026 covers January to July and is only compared with January to July of earlier years.
 Rounding in the note: the body rounds counts to the nearest thousand (or to two decimals of a million) and
   percentages to whole numbers. Appendix D lists every rounded number with its exact value and where to find it.
 Suppression: cells shown as "--" (0 to 5) are counted as 0, so totals may be slightly understated (at most 5 per
   suppressed cell: under 1% for TFWP and about 2% for IMP over the period, computed from the two CSVs). Other
   values are rounded to 5 by IRCC; sums are of rounded values.
 Canada = the 13 provinces and territories plus "province/territory not stated" (the files have no Canada row). "Not
   stated" counts in Canada but is never ranked, so provincial shares add to slightly under 100%.
 TFWP and IMP do not overlap, so "both programs" = TFWP + IMP. IRCC's IMP table covers only a subset of IMP
   permit holders: it leaves out people in Canada mainly for other reasons, such as asylum claimants.
 Figures are pictures of the Temporary Workers dashboard's own charts [13] (plain-language version, October 19,
   2026), with its card titles and source lines, taken from the dashboard in its default setting (the release above). The
   caption under each names the tab and filters. Some numbers have no chart in the dashboard (the 12-month peak
   and the latest 12-month total as a share of it, Unknown IMP as a share of all permits and of Alberta's increase, the
   change in permits other than PGWPs, and the robustness check below); they are calculated from the same two
   IRCC files (Appendix D).
 Streams are grouped from IRCC's program levels as in the dashboard (mapping in the dashboard's About the data
   tab; IRCC's own program names per group are on the Composition tab). "Non-agricultural LMIA permits" = IRCC's
   "Other Temporary Foreign Workers with LMIA" (dashboard: "Other TFWP workers with an LMIA"). "Agricultural
   permits" = the Seasonal Agricultural Worker Program plus other agricultural workers. "Unknown IMP" (IRCC does
   not identify the program; dashboard: "Unknown or other IMP") is kept separate; in this release it is entirely IRCC's
   "Unknown" program, as its other codes are suppressed cells counted as 0.
 Robustness check: leaving permits with no province or territory stated out of Canada (so Canada = the 13
   provinces and territories), B.C. supplied 12.2% of the January–July 2026 increase, against its 19.5% share of permits
   a year earlier (12.1% and 18.2% with them included), so the finding on B.C.'s share does not depend on that group.
 12-month totals (each month plus the 11 before it) remove the seasonal pattern; the peak is the highest of them
   (April 2023 to March 2024). They are calculated from the IRCC files; the dashboard's Overview cards show January–
   July 2026.
 Share of the increase = a province's change in permits divided by Canada's change over the same months, as in the
   dashboard's chart of each province's share of Canada's change (Figure 2).
 Permits vs. people: permit counts are a flow and are never used as a count of temporary workers in Canada.
   Statistics Canada's non-permanent resident estimates [12] count people living in Canada on a date, so the two can
   move in opposite directions, as they do in 2025–2026.
 Unknown IMP check: if PGWPs had only moved out of "Unknown IMP", that group would have fallen in 2026; it
   rose (checked in analysis_q2.R). Identified streams are still a lower bound for each stream, because some Unknown
   IMP permits may belong to any stream.
 Percentages are recalculated from the raw counts; the appendix tables and Appendix D show them to one decimal.
 Policy events are dated context from official sources. The data show timing, not cause. The cohort explanation for
   the 2026 PGWP rise is labelled as untested.
 Reader: a ministry manager or executive who is not a data specialist; jargon is defined on first use.

Appendix E. Reproducing the analysis
 analysis_q2.R opens the dashboard's data (IRCC open data release of September 15, 2026 (data to July 2026): the
   two CSV files, saved in ../q1/data/baseline.rds; no live download) with the dashboard's code (../q1/R/data.R,
   ../q1/R/calc.R), computes every number, and writes facts_q2.csv and the tables.
 dashboard/Mohsen_2_dashboard_shots.R starts an unchanged copy of the dashboard (commit 3c6cae6) in a
   headless browser, sets the filters named under each figure and saves the dashboard card as a picture
   (tables/figure_register.csv lists each figure's card title and filters).
 word/build_r2_docx.py builds this Word document (Mohsen_2.docx) from Mohsen's text (word/mohsen_text.md,
   used verbatim); tables/body_numbers.csv links each rounded number in it to facts_q2.csv. LibreOffice converts the
   document to Mohsen_2.pdf.
 validate_q2.R recomputes every count from the IRCC data with independent base-R code, recalculates every
   percentage from its counts, checks that every number in the body is the rounded value of its fact, checks headline
   figures against the dashboard functions, and checks the word count, page count, citations, links and the Word file.
 Every script installs any missing R packages first. The Temporary Workers dashboard:
   https://mohsenfazeli.shinyapps.io/temporary-workers-dashboard/
 AI use is described in the AI Disclosure Statement (Mohsen_AI.docx).
